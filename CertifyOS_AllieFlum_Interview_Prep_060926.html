Operating model
Q1 "Walk me through how you would design our implementation process for the next 12–18 months."
What she's probing

She's not asking about one implementation. She's asking you to architect a scalable operating model. She wants structured thinking, segmentation by customer complexity, and how you balance flexibility with standardization.

Answer direction

Start with resource audit, not segmentation. Before you can design tiered playbooks, you have to know what the machine can do: people, tools, documented process. Where is capacity thin? What is the weakest link — the person, the system, or the handoff everything else waits on? Segmentation without a resource map is a framework on paper.

Once you have the constraint map, segmentation becomes meaningful. A health plan with 10,000 providers on a 3-month fixed timeline is categorically different from a digital health company scaling into a new state. Different playbooks, different gates, different escalation paths.

The segmentation work produces something beyond routing — it produces customer type profiles. At CertifyOS's current stage, those profiles likely live in people's heads rather than transferable artifacts. Building them is part of the 12–18 month answer.

That's also where AI-enabled tooling earns its place: pattern recognition, automated intake triage matching incoming customers to the right tier, flagging risk signals early. Reproducibility without headcount scaling.

Central design question: given these resources and these customer types, where are the bottlenecks, and how do you build the playbook so the bottleneck never becomes the ceiling? Standardization at the playbook level. Flexibility at the configuration level.

If Allie asks you to be concrete about what a profile actually is: in prior work, a school's grade range, language of instruction, and curriculum type were the intake variables that shaped the entire implementation approach — resource allocation, configuration decisions, training design. The equivalent here is customer segment, roster size, and deployment model. Same discipline, different variables.

What she's probing

The transition from artisanal to systematized. Not "I ran 30 projects" — "here's how I built the framework that made 30 projects run better than the first 10."

Story — inRESONANCE portfolio model

150+ school go-lives across a 400-school portfolio is a portfolio management problem, not a project management problem.

The constraint wasn't a single calendar — it was a compound one. Legacy system expiration dates set hard outer boundaries. Schools might time their go-live around annual enrollment cycles, or they might choose a holiday or vacation window instead. The number of modules in scope (PORTAL, KEYSTONE, GENERATIONS, in various combinations) changed the complexity profile of each installation. Custom work was operationally impossible to absorb at volume without destabilizing adjacent projects.

The framework had to carry all of that: repeatable delivery structure with designed-in flex for timing variability and module count. A discipline of moving add-on features and complex customizations to post-installation — not as a concession, but as a governing principle that protected go-live integrity across the portfolio. The framework didn't eliminate variation; it contained it so variation in one school's timeline didn't cascade into the next.

A backup in one phase propagated delays across the portfolio. Treating the full set of active projects as a system, not a list — routing attention to wherever the constraint was forming before it became a bottleneck.

At CertifyOS, the anchor metrics shift to time-to-credential and time-to-network-activation — same discipline, different domain labels.

Judgment and scope
Q5 "Tell me about a time an implementation went off the rails. What happened and how did you handle it?"
What she's probing

Judgment about what's within your authority to resolve and what isn't. Discipline to contain rather than force resolution when a client's internal problem exceeds your scope.

Story — Chestnut Hill School

A fundraising implementation at an independent school surfaced a compounded problem at the sandbox milestone — the point where data conversion is complete and the client reviews their data in the new system for the first time.

A staff member in the Advancement Office, who had not been named to the project management team, objected to the co-project managers the school's own leadership had assigned. She had been shepherding fundraising data independently for some time, including solicitations run through third-party donation pages — external businesses that process donations for a cut of the proceeds and route the funds directly to the school's business office. That meant the legacy system extracts used for data conversion didn't reflect the full picture of donations received. The question wasn't just political. It was whether an accurate accounting could actually be made.

Neither issue was mine to resolve. Forcing forward on incomplete or disputed data would have been worse than pausing. I archived all available data, communications, and documentation — everything needed to reconstruct the project state — and held it until the school sorted out its internal situation. Months later the project resumed with another implementer, working from the record I'd preserved.

You can't resolve a client's internal politics. You can protect the work, document the state, and leave the next person in the best possible position.

RosterOS — where it sits in the platform

RosterOS is a normalization and routing layer that occupies two positions in the data flow depending on client type — it is not inside the credentialing pipeline itself, but it feeds and follows it.

Upstream (health plans with delegated credentialing): Health plans receive rosters from delegated entities — hospitals and clinics that credential their own providers and submit roster files to the plan. Those files arrive in inconsistent formats with errors. RosterOS standardizes, validates, and consolidates them before they reach the credentialing hub. Field matching, error detection, suggested correction, clean/unclean file retrieval. It solves the intake problem so the credentialing pipeline receives workable data.

Downstream (health systems post-credentialing): Once providers are verified and credentialed, RosterOS generates custom roster templates for submission to health plans for enrollment. Here it's an export and formatting tool — packaging verified data for payer directories, claims systems, and enrollment submissions.

Relative to the two-way API: RosterOS is primarily a self-serve web-based product, not a pure API integration. Clients who aren't API-native — who send roster files rather than programmatic feeds — can still get clean data into the CertifyOS hub via upload and validation. It bridges the gap between messy real-world data and the structured input the credentialing pipeline expects.

In plain terms: The credentialing pipeline assumes clean, structured provider data. Real clients don't have that. RosterOS is what makes their data usable before credentialing runs, and what packages the output afterward for downstream consumption.

The products
Credentialing — the original core

Automates the full provider credentialing workflow: data intake → primary source verification → committee-ready packets → decisioning → provider activation. NCQA-compliant. Headline claim: turnaround from industry standard of 28 days to under 2 minutes. Administrative expenses typically reduced 40%. Over 40,000 providers credentialed.

Licensing

Manages provider licensure across states. Primary relevance: digital health companies scaling multi-state virtual care networks where providers need licensure in multiple jurisdictions simultaneously.

Monitoring

Continuous post-credentialing surveillance. Flags license expirations, sanctions, board actions, malpractice claims. Feeds recredentialing workflows and network management. Shifts the model from episodic manual audits to real-time automated alerts.

Roster Management (RosterOS) — launched Feb 2024

Normalization and consolidation layer for provider roster data. Operates on both sides of the hub:

Left side (inbound): Cleans, validates, and consolidates inbound delegated roster files from hospitals and clinics — which arrive in inconsistent formats with errors — before they reach the hub. Field matching, error detection, suggested correction, format normalization. After the first month of implementation, uploads validate automatically.

Right side (outbound): Generates formatted roster exports for downstream systems — provider directories, claims payment systems, payer enrollment submissions. Custom export templates per health plan schema.

Bridge function: Self-serve web product that handles clients who aren't API-native. Solves the intake problem the API alone doesn't address. Supports up to 10,000 rows per file, 100 columns.

The infrastructure
CertifyOS Provider Data Hub — single source of truth

All inbound data flows in via API through a deduplication layer and into the hub: Claims Data, Rosters, Contracting & Enrollment, Primary Sourced Data, EMR & Third Party Data, Regulatory Filings, Self-Reported Data.

Health plans and other clients access verified, deduplicated provider data via outbound API exchange on the right side. The hub is the constant — all products sit on top of it or feed into it.
