Moving Customers to a New Platform
Without losing their data or their business.

If your customers need to move onto your platform from an acquired product, an older version, or a competitor's system, this is the work I do. Over 7 years I moved about 22 customers, some of them multi-site, off an acquired legacy platform onto a SaaS product, with 100% data continuity.

What was at stake

My company acquired an older low-code platform, and its customers had to move to our SaaS product. A failed migration would have put a customer at risk at the very start of a 3-year contract.

The customers were independent schools, running the same core systems as most businesses:

Admissions
works like a sales pipeline: inquiries, applications, conversions.
Enrollment
works like contracts and billing.
Fundraising
is donor management, a CRM with relationships, gifts, and campaigns.
Consortia of 5–25 schools, many multi-campus,
are multi-entity enterprise accounts.

As Implementation Manager I owned each migration end to end, running about 6 at once (10–12 at peak).

What broke

The standard path was the new platform's basic spreadsheet import, built for clean, flat data. These customers had years of customized fields, scripted workflows, and account-specific structures. The imports produced mapping errors, field mismatches, and duplicate records, which meant hours of manual cleanup on every project. Some workflows had no direct equivalent on the new platform.

The hardest case
One customer's reporting ran through a data connection to the old platform. They would not migrate until the same reports worked on the new one. I reconfigured that existing connection to feed the SaaS platform and delivered the reporting they required, and they migrated.
What I did
  1. Built a repeatable migration method to replace the basic import: field mapping, record matching, reconciliation, and a QA pass on every project.
  2. Named the gaps up front. I walked customers through the trade-offs and rebuilt their workflows, saying so when a process change beat a product change.
  3. Ran late-arriving data through the same method, including historical records and data from competitor systems.
  4. Turned recurring problems into fixes as user stories and roadmap input for Product and Engineering.
  5. Trained incoming Specialists on the method, reviewed their first migrations, and was the go-to for peers' hardest cases.
  6. Stayed with customers after launch, with 60–90 days of hypercare where needed.
