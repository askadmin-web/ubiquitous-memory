Implementation delivery · An inventory

What breaks when delivery scales

A catering service that has run two-hundred-person events does not win a two-thousand-person gala by describing its menus. It wins by naming what breaks at two thousand — that plating gives way to stations, that one kitchen becomes staging plus finishing, that direct supervision becomes a captain structure and a briefing. The food is not the question. The question is whether anyone has thought about what stops working.

Implementation delivery has the same problem. I have run six concurrent implementations as a standard load and ten to twelve at peak. Larger delivery organizations run more, across teams. The useful question is not whether the number can go up. It is which mechanisms stop working when it does, and what has to replace them.

What follows is that inventory. Five things that work at one scale, the point each one fails, and what has to take its place.

Where management enters

Each of the five has a structural half and a people half. The structural half is designable. The people half is not, and it is nested inside each item below rather than separated out — because in practice the two are not encountered separately.

The mechanism

Client stakeholders are in the room at kickoff and discovery. Requirements, decisions, owners, and data conditions are documented. Alongside that sits contingency planning — which participants are not actively involved, which are exercising judgment outside the project parameters, which dependencies rest with people who have not appeared, what happens if a committed feature or a promised data set does not arrive. The plan is real, and much of it is held and executed by the implementer personally.

Where it breaks

Not in the documentation. In participation. Sessions are missed, homework does not get done, a functional owner surfaces in week nine with data nobody disclosed, or the vendor side walks away from something the client planned around. At six to twelve accounts I absorb that — I rebuild the data set, I construct the workaround, I take the timeline hit personally. Absorption is the contingency plan, and it works because there is one of me and I am willing.

Where it stops working is when absorption is the only plan and there are forty accounts and four implementers. Personal willingness does not aggregate. It produces inconsistent recovery, invisible cost, and a delivery function whose reliability depends on how much its staff are prepared to eat.

The response

Contingency becomes structural rather than personal. Client-side obligations named as dependencies with owners and dates, not assumed as cooperation. Non-participation escalated to the executive sponsor early and as a matter of course, not as a last resort. Vendor-side commitments tracked where the client can see them. The point is not to stop absorbing — sometimes absorption is right — but to make it a decision with a visible cost rather than a reflex.

This is also where AI earns its place: tracking dependency and commitment status across a portfolio, so non-participation surfaces as a pattern early rather than as a crisis in one account. The tool does not make the call. It shortens the time before someone has to.

What it costs

Formality with clients who were fine without it, and a slower path on the accounts where quietly fixing it was genuinely the better answer. Structured contingency is worse than a good implementer's judgment on any single account and better across forty.

In advancement

A three-person shop where one person owns annual giving, events, and constituent records has few dependencies and they are all in the room. A university with advancement services sitting between separate functional owners has dependencies that cross reporting lines — and the person who can unblock one is frequently not the person in the session.

The structure names the dependency. It does not make the call at week nine on whether to absorb or escalate — and it does not decide whether an implementer who has absorbed three of these in a row is still fine. That judgment sits with a person, and the cost of getting it wrong lands on the staff first and the client second.

The mechanism

Within the hours and latitude an implementer has, they flex. An extra session for the client who needs it, a resequenced phase, a workaround the standard process does not contemplate. That discretion is real, it is legitimate, and it produces better outcomes than rigid adherence would.

Where it breaks

At enterprise volume the flex itself becomes the problem. Not because the judgment is wrong — individually it is usually right — but because it is invisible in aggregate. Capacity cannot be forecast against it, and the accounts that end up best served are the ones whose implementer had room, not the ones that needed it most.

The response

Segmentation. Explicit tiers with defined service levels, and a named exception path for accounts that genuinely do not fit — so the flex is still available but visible and costed.

What it costs

The tier is coarser than the judgment it replaces. Some clients get less than an implementer would have given them, and the exception path absorbs the ones where that matters most — imperfectly.

In advancement

Tiering by institution size is the obvious cut and frequently the wrong one. A small shop migrating thirty years of giving history is a harder engagement than a large one standing up a single campaign. Data depth and prior-system count predict effort better than headcount does.

The tier holds only if someone enforces it. Every implementer has a client they want to over-serve, usually for good reasons. Saying no to that, repeatedly, to people who are right about the client and wrong about the portfolio, is the job.

The mechanism

An issue on my account stays with me when I can resolve it. When I cannot, it routes to the department or manager who owns that resolution — development, support, management. The judgment I make is which of those two it is, and I make it fast because I hold the context.

Where it breaks

Not on my own accounts. It breaks when I become the routing judgment for accounts I do not run. The triage decision is cheap when the context is already in my head and expensive when it is not, and my availability turns into the ceiling on the whole portfolio's resolution speed.

The response

The triage judgment gets externalized into thresholds — what an implementer resolves, what routes where, what genuinely requires the most context in the room. Plus the harder half: making early escalation acceptable, because the expensive failures are the ones held too long.

What it costs

Thresholds are cruder than the judgment they encode. Some things route to the wrong place, and the correction loop is slower than one person deciding.

Thresholds are systems. Being the person who takes the call late on a Friday and does not make the implementer feel foolish for raising it is not. Escalation culture is set by how the first few are received.

The mechanism

Standards exist. QA exists — reconciliation checks, post-migration verification, defined go-live criteria. What also exists, and is not written anywhere, is the layer above them: knowing which check matters most on this data set, recognizing that a technically passing reconciliation is wrong, catching the thing no gate was designed to catch.

Where it breaks

On delegation the standard transfers and the layer above it does not. Work comes back compliant and wrong, and the gap is invisible until it surfaces at the client.

The response

Some of that judgment gets converted into the standard — the specific failure modes, the checks that only look redundant. The rest becomes review at defined gates rather than continuous oversight, with the review targeted at exactly what the written standard cannot catch.

What it costs

Written standards are coarser than judgment, and the gap never fully closes. Some things that were obviously wrong become technically compliant, and the standard gets revised after that shows up rather than before.

In advancement

A giving-history conversion reconciles or it does not — the totals are what the institution reports to its board. But a reconciliation that balances can still be wrong in ways no total exposes: soft credits attributed to the wrong constituent, pledge schedules flattened, designations collapsed. The check passes. The report is wrong.

And some of these are not QA failures at all. If the target platform has no soft-credit model, or flattens pledge schedules by design, no amount of review recovers what it cannot represent. The work at that point is disclosure rather than repair — telling the institution plainly what will not survive the move, early enough that they can plan around it.

Writing the standard is a weekend. Telling someone their work does not meet it, in a way that makes the next attempt better rather than defensive, is what determines whether the standard survives contact.

The mechanism

I know what I can hold. Six comfortably, ten to twelve at peak, and I know which mix — how many at discovery, how many at migration, how many at go-live — makes twelve possible or impossible.

Where it breaks

That knowledge is not transferable and does not aggregate. A team's capacity is not the sum of individual instincts, and without a model the answer to "can we take three more" is a guess dressed as confidence.

The response

Capacity modeled by phase rather than by count, since load is not evenly distributed across an implementation. Which turns hiring from a reaction into a forecast — and reveals the thing people miss, that adding a person subtracts capacity before it adds any.

What it costs

Models are wrong at the edges. The value is not precision, it is having something to be wrong about in a way that can be corrected.

The model says how many. It does not say who gets the account that will go badly. That decision is made by someone weighing an implementer's growth against a client's risk, and it cannot be delegated to a spreadsheet.

These are not stages of maturity. They are the same five mechanisms operating under different conditions — and at every scale, one person or several hold both halves of each: the structure, and the people executing inside it.

The structural half is what makes the other half tractable. Externalized contingency, visible tiers, defined thresholds, explicit standards — none of that removes the work of enforcing a tier or delivering hard feedback. It determines how much of that work there is. Where nothing is written down, the people half expands to fill the gap, and it expands fastest exactly when the portfolio is under the most pressure.

Which is the argument for building the structure before it is needed rather than after it is obviously missing.
